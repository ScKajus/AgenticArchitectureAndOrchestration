# Renewable Energy: Global State, 2024–2026

_Research date: 2026-10-12_

**Scope.** This report covers electricity and energy from renewable sources worldwide: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, bioenergy and marine. It also covers the storage and grids those sources depend on. Nuclear and fossil fuels appear only as comparisons. Evidence comes mainly from 2024–2026.

## Executive summary

1. **Renewables broke records again in 2025.** IRENA counts 692 GW of new renewable capacity, up from 585 GW in 2024, for a total of 5.15 TW. The IEA's figure is about 800 GW; the two count capacity differently [1][3][4].
2. **Solar is doing most of the work.** It provided about three-quarters of new capacity. In 2025 renewables produced 33.8% of global electricity, against 33.0% for coal, the first year they overtook it. Wind and solar together met 99% of the growth in electricity demand [1][7].
3. **Wind and solar remain the cheapest new power, but costs have stopped falling.** New utility-scale wind and solar are the cheapest new-build electricity almost everywhere: IRENA puts 2025 averages at $44/MWh for solar and $33/MWh for onshore wind. For the first time in years, though, BNEF's and Lazard's benchmarks rose. The causes are tariffs, interest rates and supply-chain pressure. Gas costs rose faster still [34][38][39].
4. **Batteries are the big success story.** About 307 GWh was added worldwide in 2025, up 48%. Prices for stationary battery packs fell about 45%, to around $70/kWh [49][53].
5. **The binding constraint has moved from generation to grids.** Signs of strain include:
   - a US interconnection queue of about 2 TW;
   - transformer lead times that have doubled since 2021;
   - curtailment of 15–30% in western China;
   - record hours of negative prices in Europe [55][56][57][58].
6. **Policy has split along geopolitical lines.** The US cut its tax credits and restricted offshore wind. The EU combines deployment targets with trade defence. China moved renewables onto market pricing [65][67][73][76].
7. **Supply chains are getting more concentrated.** China makes about 93.5% of the world's polysilicon and about 94% of sintered rare-earth magnets. Its suspended export controls on rare earths, graphite and batteries could return after **10 November 2026** [81][83][85].
8. **The COP28 goal of tripling capacity to about 11 TW by 2030 is off track, but the gap is narrowing.** Meeting it needs about 1,200 GW of additions a year, roughly 73% above the 2025 record. Forecasters disagree on whether that is achievable [98][102].

## Key findings

### 1. Deployment: record growth, concentrated in China, with a 2026 slowdown

**Capacity added**
- **2024:** 585 GW was added; solar was 452 GW and wind 113 GW [3].
- **2025 (IRENA):** 692 GW was added. Solar was about 511 GW, wind about 159 GW, hydro 18.4 GW, and bioenergy plus geothermal about 3.7 GW. Renewables made up 85.6% of all new power capacity [1][2].
- **The 2025 totals differ by source:**

  | Source | Measure | 2025 additions |
  |---|---|---|
  | IRENA | All renewables | 692 GW |
  | IEA | All renewables | ~800 GW |
  | IRENA | Solar (AC rating) | 511 GW |
  | SolarPower Europe | Solar (DC rating) | 664 GW |
  | IRENA | Wind | 159 GW |
  | GWEC | Wind | 165 GW |

  Sources [1][4][5][6]. Differences in AC vs DC rating and in when capacity is counted as connected probably explain most of the gap.

**Share of generation**
- Ember finds renewables passed coal in 2025: 33.8% of generation against 33.0% [7].
- Solar output rose a record 636 TWh (+30%) and overtook wind [7].
- Fossil-fuel generation fell 0.2% [7].
- In April 2026, wind and solar together produced more electricity than gas worldwide for the first time in a single month: 22% against 20% [8].
- In the EU in 2025, wind and solar together (30.1%) produced more electricity than all fossil fuels combined (29.0%) [9].
- Electricity is not all energy use. Modern renewables supplied only about 13.4% of total final energy in 2023, or 18% including traditional biomass. Heat and transport lag far behind the power sector [10].

**Where the growth is**
- China added about 440 GW in 2025, about 64% of the world total [2].
- China, the US and the EU together accounted for 79.5% of additions [2].
- India became the world's #2 solar market. It added a record 55.3 GW of non-fossil capacity in FY2025-26 [2][13].
- The Middle East grew fastest (+28.9%), and Africa added only 11.3 GW [1].
- Imports of cheap Chinese panels point to a lot of distributed solar that official statistics probably miss:
  - Africa: 15 GW in 12 months, up 60% [14].
  - Pakistan: 16.6 GW in 2024 [15].

**2026 so far**
- China's solar installations from January to July 2026 were 61% lower than a year earlier [11].
- That comparison is distorted by a rush to install in May 2025 before China's pricing reform took effect.
- SolarPower Europe forecasts the first annual fall in global solar installations in more than 20 years: about 612 GW, down 8% [5].
- The US is still growing. It installed 11.4 GW of solar in Q2 2026, up 45% year on year, as developers raced the tax-credit deadline [12].

### 2. Technology: solar efficiency keeps climbing; wind is splitting; geothermal arrives

**Solar**
- **Lab records:**
  - Silicon cell: 27.81% (LONGi's HIBC back-contact design, certified by ISFH, April 2025) [17].
  - Perovskite-silicon tandem cell: 35.5% (LONGi, certified by ESTI, July 2026) [16].
- **Commercial modules:**
  - The industry is moving from TOPCon, at about 23.7%, to back-contact designs at 24.7–25%.
  - TOPCon still holds about 70% of new manufacturing capacity [18].
- **Tandem modules are only at early commercial stage:**
  - Oxford PV made the first commercial tandem shipments in 2024 [20].
  - Trina's full-size tandem module was certified above 800 W in 2025 [19].
  - Long-term durability in the field is still unproven. Oxford PV is aiming for a 20-year lifetime only by 2028 [20].

**Wind**
- **Turbine size:** China's Dongfang Electric installed a 26 MW offshore prototype [21]. Western manufacturers' commercial turbines remain around 15 MW [22].
- **Western industry under strain:**
  - Siemens Gamesa found quality defects across about 2,900 onshore turbines [23].
  - Ørsted needed a DKK 60bn rights issue after US stop-work orders [24].
- **Floating offshore wind:** no new capacity was commissioned worldwide in 2025, the first such year since 2015. The global total was 278 MW (GWEC) [25].
- **All offshore wind:** 9.3 GW was added in 2025, bringing the total to 92.5 GW [26].

**Geothermal**
- Fervo's Cape Station in Utah began commercial operation on 30 September 2026, at 33 MW net. It is the first greenfield enhanced geothermal system to do so [27].
- Fervo says the rest of Phase I (about 100 MW in total) is due by January 2027, and it reports more than 1 GW of signed power contracts. These are company-reported figures [28].
- Other approaches are still at pilot stage:
  - Eavor's closed-loop system in Germany [29].
  - Quaise's millimetre-wave drilling [30].

**Hydro and marine**
- Pumped storage hydro added a record of about 11.6 GW in 2025, taking the world total past 200 GW. China accounts for about 218 GW of the 243 GW under construction [32].
- Tidal stream remains tiny: 16.2 GWh generated worldwide in 2025, more than 80% of it from MeyGen in Scotland [33].

### 3. Economics: still cheapest, but costs have stopped falling

**Cost levels.** The table shows the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) from three sources, in US dollars per MWh.

| Technology | IRENA, 2025 global average [34] | BNEF, 2025 benchmark [38] | Lazard, 2026 US unsubsidized [39] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Utility solar PV | 44 | 39 (+6%) | ~40–98 |
| Onshore wind | 33 (−4%) | 40 (+2%) | ~37–99 |
| Offshore wind | 78 (−3%) | 100 (+12%) | ~105–167 |
| New gas, combined-cycle | ~50–100, depending on gas price | 102 (+16%, a record) | ~51–129 |
| 4-hour battery | — | 78 (−27%) | storage cost 210–292 |

- IRENA finds that more than 90% of new utility-scale renewables beat the cheapest new fossil option [34].
- Solar paired with storage at good sites now costs under $85/MWh as "firm" power, i.e. available 95% of the time. That is down from more than $100/MWh in 2020 [34].

**Why costs rose in 2025–26**
- Financing costs and high interest rates [35][39].
- US tariffs and the cuts to US tax credits [39].
- A shortage of gas turbines, which is pushing up gas costs [38].
- Offshore wind cost inflation:
  - In the UK, the weighted-average guaranteed price for fixed-bottom offshore wind in the 2026 contracts-for-difference auction (AR7) was £90.91/MWh, against £37.35/MWh in AR4 (in 2012 prices) [47].
  - Ørsted booked a $1.7bn impairment on its Sunrise Wind project [48].

**Investment**
- Energy-transition investment reached a record $2.3T in 2025, up 8% (BNEF) [42].
- Investment in renewable generation itself fell about 9.5%, to $690bn. China's market reform drove the fall [42].
- The IEA estimates clean-energy investment at about $2.2T a year, against about $1.1–1.2T for fossil fuels [43][45].
- Africa receives about 2% of global clean-energy investment despite having about 20% of the world's population [44].
- In emerging and developing economies, capital for renewables costs two to three times as much as in advanced economies. This is the main barrier there [46].

### 4. Grids and storage: batteries scale up; grids are the bottleneck

**Batteries**
- BNEF counts 112 GW / 307 GWh added in 2025, up 48% [49]:
  - China took 54% of additions and the US 16%.
  - 90% of the batteries used lithium iron phosphate (LFP) chemistry.
  - BNEF expects 459 GWh in 2026.
- The US installed a record 57.6 GWh in 2025 [51].
- Texas's grid (ERCOT) is overtaking California's (CAISO) in battery capacity [52].
- Chinese sources disagree on that country's additions because they define storage differently [50].

**Battery prices**
- The average lithium-ion pack price was $108/kWh [53].
- Stationary-storage packs were about $70/kWh, down 45% [53].
- Complete installed systems differ widely by region: about $73/kWh in China, $177/kWh in Europe and $219/kWh in the US [54].

**Grid bottlenecks**
- **US interconnection queue:** about 2.06 TW at end-2025. Of the capacity that applied in 2000–2019, only 13% was ever built [55].
- **Equipment:** large transformers take up to 4 years to procure, about twice as long as in 2021 [56].
- **Projects waiting:** about 1,650 GW of advanced wind and solar projects are waiting for grid connection worldwide [56].
- **China curtailment:**
  - Solar curtailment reached 6.6% in H1 2025 [57].
  - Northern and western export regions see 15–30% curtailment [57].
  - China raised its allowed ceiling from 5% to 10% [57].
  - It stopped publishing utilization rates by source in 2024 [57].
- **Negative prices in Europe:** Germany had 573 hours of negative prices in 2025, up 25%. Spain's roughly doubled [58].

**The Iberian blackout (28 April 2025)**
- The Spanish grid operator (REE) and the power utilities blamed each other [59].
- ENTSO-E's final report (March 2026) found insufficient voltage and reactive-power control. Contributing factors were:
  - conventional generators under-delivering reactive power;
  - renewable plants on fixed power-factor settings, some of which tripped early [60].
- ENTSO-E's board chair: _"The problem is not renewable energy, but voltage control."_ [60]

**Other ways to add flexibility**
- **Interconnectors:** the €1.6bn Bay of Biscay link between Spain and France is due in 2028 [64].
- **Grid-forming batteries:** in Australia these are starting to replace synchronous condensers for grid stability. One contract cost about $9,960/MVA per year, against about $77,275 for synchronous condensers [62].
- **EU batteries and demand flexibility:** Ember projects the EU battery fleet growing from about 37 GW (2025) to 178 GW (2030) [63].
- **Long-duration storage:** includes Form Energy's iron-air batteries. Evidence here is weak and secondary [61].

### 5. Policy and geopolitics: a three-way split

**United States**
- **Tax credits:** the 2025 reconciliation law (OBBBA) ends the main wind and solar credits early. Projects had to start construction by about July 2026, or be in service by the end of 2027. The law also adds "foreign entity of concern" restrictions [65].
- **Treasury rules:** guidance removed the 5% cost safe harbor for most wind and solar projects [66].
- **Offshore wind halts:**
  - In August 2025 the Interior Department halted Revolution Wind.
  - In December 2025 it halted all five large projects under construction.
  - All five developers won preliminary injunctions.
  - In April 2026 two leases were bought back in return for investment in fossil fuels [67][68].
- **Tariffs:**
  - Anti-dumping and countervailing duties on solar cells from Southeast Asia, reaching up to 3,400% [69].
  - A Section 232 tariff of 15% on polysilicon and solar products, with minimum import prices, effective 4 December 2026 [70].
- **Lost investment:** E2 tracks $68.2bn of clean-energy investment cancelled from January 2025 to May 2026 [71].

**European Union**
- RED III sets a target of 42.5% renewables by 2030. Designation of "acceleration areas" for faster permitting is lagging [72].
- The Net-Zero Industry Act requires that at least 30% of renewable auction volume uses non-price criteria such as resilience and cybersecurity [73].
- The carbon border tax (CBAM) entered its definitive phase in 2026 [74].
- Since May 2026, EU development banks no longer fund projects that use "high-risk" Chinese inverters [75].

**China**
- **Document 136 (2025):** new wind and solar now sell at market prices, backed by a contract-for-difference-style guarantee. By October 2025 only half of provinces had finalised their rules [76].
- **2035 climate pledge (NDC):** China's first absolute emissions target, a 7–10% cut from peak, and about 3,600 GW of wind and solar. Climate Action Tracker calls the target a floor [77].
- **Export rebates:** China is ending its export VAT rebates for solar products (April 2026) and batteries (January 2027) to ease trade friction [78].

**Climate summits**
- **COP29** agreed a climate-finance goal of $300bn a year by 2035, plus pledges on storage and grids [79].
- **COP30** adopted no roadmap for moving away from fossil fuels, but set up the first UN climate dialogues on trade measures [80].

### 6. Supply chains, environment and society

**Concentration**
- China produces 93.5% of the world's polysilicon [81].
- The top three refiners' average market share across key minerals rose from 82% (2020) to 86% (2024) [82].
- China accounts for 91% of refined magnet rare earths and 94% of sintered permanent magnets, which are used in direct-drive and offshore wind turbines [83].
- China holds about 85% of battery cell manufacturing capacity [84].

**China's export controls**
- **April 2025:** controls on rare earths and magnets. These remain in force [85][86].
- **October 2025:** much broader controls covering rare earths, graphite and batteries. These are suspended only until 10 November 2026 [85][86].
- The IEA reports that about $6.5T a year of production outside China would be at high risk if the full rare-earth controls were applied. This figure comes from secondary coverage [87].

**Environmental impacts**
- **Lifecycle emissions:** wind produces about 8–23 gCO₂e/kWh and solar PV about 8–83, against about 400–510 for gas and 750–1,100 for coal (UNECE) [88].
- **Biodiversity:** a 2025 working paper on about 41,000 plants finds that renewables' biodiversity impact is rising. Most of the harm comes from the worst-sited 1% of plants [89].
- **Mining:** nickel mining in Indonesia has caused deforestation and water contamination [90].
- **Waste:** projected waste by 2050 is 60–78 Mt of solar panels and 43 Mt of wind blades. Both projections predate 2018 [91][92].
- **Recycling:** battery recycling is as concentrated in China as primary refining [93].

**Social impacts**
- **Forced labour:** the US added five Chinese solar suppliers to its Uyghur forced-labour (UFLPA) list [94].
- **Child labour in DRC cobalt:** estimates conflict sharply [95].
- **US local opposition:**
  - 865 local and 23 state-level restrictions.
  - A record 9.8 GW of cancelled projects in 2025.
  - The Sabin Center attributes much of this to misinformation [96].
- **Jobs:** renewables employ 16.6 million people (2024), but growth has slowed to 2.3% [97].

### 7. Outlook to 2030–2035

**The tripling goal**
- **IRENA (September 2026):** reaching 11.2 TW by 2030 needs about 1,200 GW of additions a year. IRENA says the gap has "narrowed", but warns that growth may slow and that grids are lagging. It says grid investment needs to be about $1T a year [98].
- **IEA (Renewables 2025):** its main case adds about 4,600 GW in 2025–2030, roughly a doubling. It cut its US forecast in half because of OBBBA [100][101].
- **Ember:** tripling is "within reach" if additions grow about 12% a year [102].
- **National targets** add up to only about 7.8 TW [103].
- China's solar industry association expects China's 2026 solar installations to fall 24–43% [104].

**Long-term forecasts**
- **IEA World Energy Outlook 2025:** renewables grow fastest in every scenario. Even under current policies they reach just under half of generation by 2035. Solar averages about 540 GW a year to 2035 [105][106].
- **BNEF New Energy Outlook:**
  - 2025: renewables reach about 67% of power by 2050 [108].
  - 2026: solar becomes the largest single source of generation by about 2032. Storage grows about 17-fold. BNEF also sees a "dash to gas" driven by data centres [107].
- **DNV:** renewables reach 65% of electricity by 2040, but the primary energy mix is still half fossil in 2050 [109].
- **BP, ExxonMobil and OPEC:** much more conservative. BP has renewables at about a quarter of primary energy by 2050, and ExxonMobil has oil and gas above half [110][111].

**What to watch**
- **AI and data centres:** the IEA projects data-centre demand rising from about 415 TWh to about 945 TWh by 2030 [112].
- **Electrification:** how fast transport, heating and industry switch to electricity.
- **Policy reversals:** in the US and elsewhere.
- **Cost of capital:** especially in developing economies.
- **Grid build-out.**
- **China's export controls:** whether they return after 10 November 2026.

## Points of disagreement

- **How much was installed.** For 2025, IRENA says 692 GW, the IEA about 800 GW and Ember 793 GW (estimated). For solar alone, IRENA says 511 GW (AC rating) and SolarPower Europe 664 GW (DC rating). These gaps make the tripling goal look closer or further away depending on the source [1][4][5][102].
- **Which way costs are moving.** For wind in 2025, IRENA shows costs falling and BNEF shows them rising. IRENA averages projects that were commissioned, while BNEF benchmarks projects being financed and weights China differently. For batteries, BNEF and IRENA show record-low costs worldwide, while Lazard shows US storage costs up 27% since 2020 because of US tariffs and foreign-entity rules [34][38][40].
- **What the long-term "share" measures.** BNEF, the IEA and DNV give renewables 50–67% of electricity by 2040–2050. Oil companies and OPEC give 15–27% of primary energy. Much of the apparent disagreement comes from this difference in measure [105][108][109][110].
- **How much US policy matters globally.** The IEA halved its US forecast, while DNV calls the global effect "marginal" [101][109].
- **Who caused the Iberian blackout.** The grid operator and the utilities still blame each other. ENTSO-E points to failures in voltage control across the system [59][60].
- **Floating wind totals.** GWEC reports 278 MW at end-2025, and RenewableUK reports 382 MW in October 2026. They use different datasets [25].

## Open questions and limitations

- **Primary sources were often blocked.** Many IEA pages returned HTTP 403, and the Lazard 2026 and BNEF NEO 2026 PDFs could not be read. Figures from those reports come from trade-press coverage and should be checked against the originals.
- **No full-year 2026 data yet.** Whether global additions actually fall in 2026 is unknown.
- **Renewables' share of total final energy** is available only up to 2023.
- **China's curtailment** has no official full-year figure, because China stopped publishing utilization rates by source.
- **Company-reported figures are not independently verified.** This applies to LONGi, Fervo and Oxford PV, except where a named lab certified the result.
- **Long-duration storage deployment** rests only on weak secondary sources.
- **Waste projections for solar panels and wind blades** are from 2016–2017. Data on DRC child labour is stale or comes from advocacy groups.
- **Near-term events to watch:**
  - whether China reactivates its export controls after 10 November 2026;
  - the US Section 232 polysilicon tariffs taking effect on 4 December 2026;
  - the IEA's Renewables 2026 report, expected around October 2026.
