# Volcanoes

_Research date: 2026-10-13_

## Executive summary

**Where volcanoes are.** Volcanoes form where mantle rock melts and the melt rises. Decompression melting happens at mid-ocean ridges, rifts and hotspots, and flux melting at subduction zones. Ridges produce most of Earth's magma by volume (about 70–80%), almost all of it underwater. Subduction zones host about 70% of the catalogued volcanoes on land [9][10][16].

**What controls eruption style.** Magma silica and gas content control how explosive an eruption is. Fluid basalt feeds effusive Hawaiian and Strombolian activity. Viscous andesite, dacite and rhyolite trap gas and drive Vulcanian, Peléan and Plinian explosions. Eruption size is measured on the 0–8 Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) [5][7][13].

**How active the planet is.** About 1,350 volcanoes are potentially active [1]. The Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program (GVP) recorded 66 volcanoes active in 2024 and 63 in 2025, with 29–31 new eruptions starting each year. GVP sees no real increase in volcanism; recent counts rise because detection has improved [2].

**Notable recent activity (2024–2026):**
- Iceland's Sundhnúkur eruption series near Grindavík [17].
- Kīlauea's record-breaking episodic lava fountains [20].
- Deadly Indonesian events: the Lewotobi eruption in Nov 2024, Marapi lahars in May 2024, and the Dukono eruption in May 2026 [24][27][36].
- A first-recorded eruption at Hayli Gubbi in Ethiopia [33].
- A new submarine volcano, Titan Ridge, in Papua New Guinea [38].
- Escalating unrest at Campi Flegrei near Naples, including a Md 4.7 earthquake in July 2026 [43].

**Deaths and exposure.** Since 1500, volcanoes have killed about 278,000 people. Most of the deaths came from pyroclastic density currents, lahars, tsunamis and post-eruption famine [48][49]. Roughly 800 million to more than 1 billion people live within 100 km of a Holocene volcano [52][53].

**Wider impacts.** Large sulfur-rich eruptions cool the climate for 1–3 years. Pinatubo cooled it by about 0.5 °C [62][63]. Human CO₂ emissions exceed volcanic emissions by more than 100 times [64][65]. Volcanic ash is a recognised hazard to jet engines, managed through nine Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres [71][74].

**Monitoring and forecasting.** Forecasting works well at frequently erupting, densely instrumented volcanoes such as Pinatubo and Iceland's Svartsengi. It still fails for sudden steam-driven blasts and remote submarine eruptions [56][83][84][85]. Fewer than half of Holocene volcanoes have even one monitoring station [89].

**Benefits.** Geothermal capacity reached 17.2 GW at the end of 2025 [95], and the first commercial enhanced-geothermal plant came online in Utah in October 2026 [101]. Volcanic soils, ore deposits and tourism also bring large benefits [103][105][109].

**Outlook.** The most plausible global-scale threat is not a "supervolcano" but a VEI 7 eruption, estimated at about 1-in-6 per century. Experts argue the world is underprepared for one [118]. Risk-reduction policy tends to be reactive and funded after crises [92][121].

## Key findings

### 1. How volcanoes form and erupt

**Tectonic settings**
- **Decompression melting:** hot mantle rises and melts as pressure drops beneath mid-ocean ridges, continental rifts and hotspots, making basaltic magma [7].
- **Flux melting:** at subduction zones, water released from the sinking slab lowers the melting point of the mantle wedge above it [7].
- **Ridges by volume:** about 75% of global volcanism (range 70–80%) is at mid-ocean ridges, mostly unobserved on the deep seafloor [9][16]. Crisp (1984) put total magma production at about 4–5 km³/yr, with convergent margins contributing about 15% of output [16].
- **Subduction by count:** a breakdown of 1,430 GVP Holocene volcanoes finds about 70% in subduction settings, about 14% in rifts and about 13% intraplate [10].
- **Volume and count measure different things.** Rift volcanoes are under-counted because submarine ridge eruptions go largely uncatalogued [10].

**Magma types**

| Magma | SiO₂ (wt%) | Temperature (°C) | Viscosity (Pa·s) | Gas content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basaltic | 45–55 | 1,000–1,200 | 10–10³ | low |
| Andesitic | 55–65 | 800–1,000 | 10³–10⁵ | intermediate |
| Dacitic | ~62–69 | — | high | high |
| Rhyolitic | 65–75 | 650–800 | 10⁵–10⁹ | high |

- Sources for the table: [7][14][15]. Rhyolite temperatures are also given as 750–850 °C [14].
- Water vapour is the dominant volcanic gas, followed by CO₂ and smaller amounts of sulfur, chlorine and fluorine species [14].
- In fluid magma, bubbles escape freely. In viscous magma they stay trapped until the magma fragments explosively. "Explosive eruptions are favored by high gas content and high viscosity" [7][15].

**Eruption styles**
- **Hawaiian:** lava fountains and fissure-fed flows. Fissure eruptions also build lava plateaus such as Iceland and the Columbia Plateau [5][11].
- **Strombolian:** glowing lava clots thrown out in arcs, typically VEI 1–2, building cinder cones [5][13].
- **Vulcanian:** dense, ash-laden explosions, typically VEI 2–4 [5][13].
- **Peléan:** dome collapse feeding pyroclastic flows. Mont Pelée destroyed St. Pierre in 1902 [5].
- **Plinian:** eruption columns tens of kilometres high, as at St. Helens in 1980 and Pinatubo in 1991 [5].
- **Phreatic and phreatomagmatic:** driven by water. Phreatic blasts erupt only steam and old rock. Surtseyan and phreatoplinian eruptions involve magma meeting water [5][8].

**Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)**
- Newhall and Self defined it in 1982. It is based mainly on erupted volume [13]:
  - VEI 4: at least 0.1 km³
  - VEI 6: at least 10 km³
  - VEI 8: at least 1,000 km³
- Each step from VEI 2 upward is a tenfold increase [13].
- Approximate recurrence: a VEI 4 about every 18 months, a VEI 6 every 50–100 years, a VEI 7 every 500–1,000 years, and a VEI 8 less often than once per 50,000 years [13].
- VEI ignores deposit density and SO₂, so it is a weak guide to climate impact [13].
- The VEI table here comes from a secondary summary because the primary GVP page could not be fetched.

**Volcano types**
- **Shield volcanoes:** basaltic, slopes under 10°, the largest by volume. Mauna Loa rises more than 9 km from the seafloor [6][11].
- **Stratovolcanoes:** steep cones of alternating lava and tephra, the typical subduction-zone volcano [6][11].
- **Cinder cones:** usually erupt only once (monogenetic) [11][12].
- **Lava domes:** viscous dacite or rhyolite piled over the vent [11][15].
- **Calderas:** collapse depressions formed after large eruptions. Tambora's caldera is 6 km wide and 1.1 km deep [6][12].
- Classification is not uniform. The British Geological Survey (BGS) recognises two basic types, while USGS and the National Park Service (NPS) list three to four or more [6][11][12].

### 2. The global landscape and notable eruptions, 2024–2026

**Counts**

| Measure | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Potentially active volcanoes | ~1,350 | USGS [1] |
| Volcanoes with eruptions in historical time | ~500 | USGS [1] |
| Potentially active volcanoes in the US | ~170 | [1] |
| Volcanoes active in a year | 66 (2024), 63 (2025), 62–82 per year since 2000 | GVP [2] |
| Eruptions in a year | 74 (2024), 71 (2025) | GVP [2] |
| New eruptions started | 31 (2024), 29 (2025) | GVP [2] |

- The often-quoted "50–70 eruptions per year" matches 1960s–1990s counts and has no primary source. An older USGS publication says "about 50 or so" [2][4].
- At any time, 40–50 volcanoes are in "continuing eruption" status: 45 at 30 Dec 2025 and 40 at 31 Mar 2026. About 20 are erupting on any given day [3].
- GVP's top five countries by number of Holocene volcanoes are the US, Japan, Indonesia, Russia and Chile [3].

**Iceland, Reykjanes Peninsula**
- Nine eruptions on the Sundhnúkur crater row between December 2023 and July–August 2025. They forced repeated evacuations of Grindavík and the Blue Lagoon [17][18][19].
- Magma has kept accumulating beneath Svartsengi: about 29.1 million m³ by August 2026 [17].
- No eruption had occurred by 30 September 2026. The Icelandic Met Office (IMO) still calls an intrusion or eruption "most likely", with only 20 minutes to about 4 hours of warning [17].

**Kīlauea, Hawaiʻi**
- An episodic lava-fountaining eruption began in Halemaʻumaʻu crater on 23 December 2024 [20].
- Episode 48, on 1 June 2026, broke Puʻuʻōʻō's record of 47 episodes [20].
- Fountains reached about 290 m in July 2026, and episode 54 occurred on 25 August 2026 [20].

**Etna, Italy**
- Frequent paroxysms in 2025 [21].
- A summit eruption began on 6 August 2026, with lava fountaining and several new effusive vents [21]:
  - The aviation colour code was red from 6–7 August to 18 August.
  - The ash plume reached about 6 km on 17 August.
  - About 5.6–7.6 million m³ of lava was erupted.
- Catania airport closed repeatedly [22][23]. Ash reached Malta and Libya [22].
- Reported cancellations range from about 388 flights (Euronews, 12 August) to about 700 flights and 100,000 passengers affected (by 13 August, citing the airport operator SAC) [22][23].

**Indonesia**
- **Lewotobi Laki-laki:** explosive eruptions in November 2024 killed 9–10 people, displaced more than 4,000 and disrupted flights to Bali [24]. An eruption on 7 July 2025 sent ash about 18 km high [24]. Frequent eruptions continued in 2026 [25].
- **Ruang:** eruptions in April 2024 closed Manado airport and led to the evacuation of 6,000–12,000 people. A tsunami warning was issued because of the 1871 precedent [26].
- **Marapi:** rain-triggered lahars on 11–12 May 2024 killed at least 67 people [27].
- **Semeru:** pyroclastic density currents in November 2025 ran up to about 7 km. The alert went to its highest level and about 1,000 people were evacuated [32].
- **Dukono:** on 8 May 2026 an ash column rose 10 km above the summit [36][37]:
  - It killed three climbers (two Singaporeans and one Indonesian), all buried by tephra within 50 m of the crater rim.
  - Climbing had been banned since 17 April.
  - Reports differ on the injured: 3 (disaster agency BNPB) or 5 (police and AP).

**Philippines**
- **Kanlaon:** eruptions in June and December 2024 and April–May 2025 displaced up to about 28,000 people at a time and disrupted flights [28]. A moderately explosive eruption on 10 October 2026 sent ash 5–6 km above the crater, with no casualties [29].
- **Mayon:** the volcano agency PHIVOLCS raised the alert to Level 3 on 6 January 2026 after a dome collapse produced pyroclastic density currents, and about 3,500 people were sheltered [30].
  - Lava flows reached 2.7–3.8 km.
  - The alert was lowered to Level 2 on 5 August 2026 [30][31].
  - One first-round source wrongly described Level 3 as running through October.

**Other notable events**
- **Hayli Gubbi, Ethiopia (23 November 2025):** the first eruption on record, with an ash plume of about 14–15 km [33][34]:
  - About 220,000 t of SO₂.
  - Ash drifted to India and China, and Air India and other carriers cancelled flights.
  - No deaths.
  - Satellite plume estimates run higher, at 16–20 km [33].
- **Krasheninnikov, Kamchatka (3 August 2025):** erupted days after an M8.8 offshore earthquake. It was the first eruption in an estimated 450–600 years [35]. A causal link to the earthquake is plausible but unproven [35].
- **Titan Ridge, Papua New Guinea (from about 8 May 2026):** a submarine eruption in the Bismarck Sea [38][39][40]:
  - Plumes reached up to 5.5 km.
  - Pumice rafts struck the Admiralty Islands.
  - More than 10,000 fishing-dependent residents were affected, according to the UN migration agency (IOM).
  - Activity was very low by July [38].
- **Fuego, Guatemala (2–5 August 2026):** lava fountains, pyroclastic flows travelling 5–7 km, and 1,522 people in shelters [41].

**Unrest without eruption**
- **Campi Flegrei:**
  - Continuous uplift since 2005 [42][43].
  - Md 4.6 earthquakes in March and June 2025 [42].
  - A Md 4.7 on 31 July 2026 (about Mw 4.4), the strongest recorded there. It caused injuries (counts vary from 8 to 21), power cuts and rail suspensions [43].
- **Santorini–Amorgos:** a swarm in January–June 2025 produced more than 21,000 earthquakes, several above M5 [44]. More than 11,000 people left the island. Scientists interpreted a dike intrusion and said no eruption was imminent [44].
- **Mount Spurr, Alaska:** unrest in 2024–2025 stalled. The alert was lowered to Normal on 20 August 2025 [45].
- **Axial Seamount:** the forecast eruption window slipped to mid-to-late 2026. It had not erupted at last check [46][47].

### 3. Hazards to people

**Fatality records**
- Brown et al. (2017) count 278,368 deaths in 635 incidents from 1500 to 2017 [48].
- Auker et al. (2013) count about 279,000 deaths from 1600 to 2010 [49]. Pyroclastic density currents (PDCs) and lahars together caused about half [49].
- A few disasters dominate the totals. Most fatal incidents killed fewer than ten people [48].

**Deadliest hazards**
- **PDCs:** about 60,000 deaths in 102 incidents. The UN disaster risk office (UNDRR) calls them the deadliest direct hazard [50].
- **Lahars:** about 50,000 deaths. Including secondary lahars and debris avalanches, landslide-type flows caused about 60,000 [51].
- **Volcanic tsunamis:** Krakatau in 1883 alone killed about 36,000 [48].
- **Famine and disease:** about 62,600 indirect deaths, for example after Tambora in 1815 [48].
- **PDC share depends on the method:** estimates are 33% (Auker, 1600–2010), 28% (1500–2017) or about 21% (Brown's raw counts) [49][50].

**Distance and timing**
- Ballistics dominate deaths within 5 km of the vent, PDCs at 5–15 km, and lahars, tsunamis and ash beyond 15 km [48].
- Tourists (561 deaths) and scientists (67) died mostly very close to vents [48].
- Death tolls track population density and hazard type more than VEI. Vulnerability has fallen over two centuries [49].

**Population exposure**

| Distance from a Holocene volcano | People (2011 data) |
|---|---|
| Within 10 km | ~29 million |
| Within 30 km | ~226 million |
| Within 100 km | ~800 million |

- These figures are from the Global Volcano Model study (GVM) [52].
- With 2015 data, Freire et al. put more than 1 billion people (14% of humanity) within 100 km [53].
- Meredith et al. (2025) count about 431 million city dwellers within 100 km [54]. Jakarta has the largest exposed population, at about 38 million. Naples and Managua have the largest shares of their populations within 10 km [54].

**Lessons from disasters**
- **Armero, 1985:** about 23,000–25,000 died. Monitoring and a draft hazard map existed, but there was no clear warning chain, even though lahars took about 2 hours to arrive [55].
- **Pinatubo, 1991:** a joint USGS–PHIVOLCS forecast led to the evacuation of about 65,000–75,000 people [56]. USGS credits it with saving at least 5,000 lives and US$250 million; other estimates say 20,000 lives [56].
- **Whakaari/White Island, 2019:** 22 of 47 visitors died [57]:
  - Tour operators were convicted.
  - The island owner's conviction was quashed in February 2025. The judge said the victims "should never have been there."
- **Marapi, 2023:** 23–24 climbers died. Climbing permits were issued despite an alert at Level II with a 3 km exclusion zone, and the Ombudsman found maladministration [58].
- **Common lesson:** warnings work only when they are linked to communication and enforced access rules [55][58].

**Economic losses**
- Volcanic losses are small globally but can be severe regionally [60]. The 2015 Global Assessment Report (GAR 2015) estimates average annual ash-damage losses to buildings at more than US$11 billion in Japan and about US$6 billion in Indonesia [60].
- **Hunga Tonga, 2022:** damage of US$90 million (18.5% of GDP), later revised to US$182 million (more than 36% of GDP) [59].
- **La Palma, 2021:** €842–900+ million in damage and about 1,345 homes destroyed [61].

### 4. Climate, aviation and environmental impacts

**Climate**
- Stratospheric SO₂ forms reflective sulfate aerosol.
  - **Pinatubo:** about 15–20 Mt SO₂. It cooled global temperatures by about 0.5 °C (peaking near 0.7 °C) for 1–3 years [62][63].
  - **Tambora, 1815:** caused the 1816 "year without a summer" [62].
  - **Laki, 1783–84:** about 120 Mt SO₂ [62].
- **Volcanic CO₂ is small** next to human emissions: about 0.13–0.44 Gt/yr from volcanoes against more than 35–41 Gt/yr from humans, which is more than 100 times as much [64][65].
- **Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai (2022) was unprecedented.** It injected about 146–150 Tg of water vapour, roughly 10% of the stratosphere's water, but only about 0.4–0.5 Tg of SO₂ [66][69]. About half of the water remained by mid-2025 [70].
- **Its net climate effect has been revised:**
  - Early water-vapour-only modelling projected slight warming, up to about 0.035 °C [67].
  - A 2025 observational study that includes sulfate and ozone finds net Southern Hemisphere cooling of about −0.1 K in 2022–23. It measured radiative forcing at the top of the atmosphere (TOA) of −0.55 W m⁻² in 2022 [68].

**Aviation**
- Ash melts in jet engines. British Airways Flight 9 lost all four engines over Galunggung in 1982 and recovered [71].
- **Eyjafjallajökull, 2010:** more than 100,000 flights were cancelled, costing airlines about US$1.7 billion [71][72].
- **Rules changed after 2010.** Before, the rule was zero ash tolerance. Afterwards, concentration thresholds of 0.2, 2 and 4 mg/m³ were introduced. ICAO's newer quantitative volcanic ash (QVA) products are recommended for all centres from November 2026 [73].
- **Nine ICAO Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres (VAACs):** Anchorage, Montréal, Washington, Buenos Aires, London, Toulouse, Tokyo, Darwin and Wellington [74].
- Disruption from 2024–2026 eruptions (Ruang, Lewotobi, Hayli Gubbi, Etna) shows that the risk persists [22][24][26][34].

**Environment**
- **Gases:** vog and acid rain from SO₂, HCl and HF, and lethal CO₂ pooling in low ground [75].
- **Laki:** about 8 Mt of fluorine killed roughly 80% of Iceland's sheep and 50% of its cattle and horses [76]. A 2024 study attributes the human deaths mainly to the famine that followed [76].
- **Ecosystem recovery:** at Mount St Helens it has been complex and seeded by survivors [139].
- **Ocean fertilisation:** ash triggered large phytoplankton blooms after Kasatochi in 2008 and Hunga Tonga in 2022 [77][78]. The carbon drawdown was inefficient, about 0.01 Pg C for Kasatochi [77].

### 5. Monitoring and eruption forecasting

**Techniques**
- **Seismic and infrasound:** best used together. Infrasound confirms explosions quickly [81].
- **Ground deformation:** measured with GNSS, tiltmeters and InSAR. A 2026 review of a decade of Sentinel-1 radar (Biggs et al.) analysed 3.3 million interferograms [79]. Only 90 of 233 high-priority volcanoes appear in peer-reviewed Sentinel-1 studies [79].
- **Gas:** MultiGAS instruments on the ground [81], and TROPOMI SO₂ retrievals from satellite [80].
  - A rising CO₂/SO₂ ratio preceded Villarrica's 2015 paroxysm [82].
  - No validated threshold exists [82].
- **Drones:** used for gas and topography surveys [81].

**Successes**
- **Pinatubo, 1991:** see section 3 [56].
- **Iceland, 2023–2025:** IMO's magma-volume thresholds anticipated Sundhnúkur eruptions [18][88].
  - Timing errors were common.
  - The required volume rose over time [18][88].
  - In 2026, accumulation exceeded the earlier upper threshold of about 23 million m³ without an eruption. That raises questions about the method [17][88].

**Failures**
- **Ontake, 2014:** a phreatic eruption that killed at least 58–63 hikers. The alert stayed at Level 1, and precursors appeared only about 10 minutes before [83].
- **Whakaari, 2019:** experts had put the eruption probability at 8–14% within four weeks, and the alert stayed at Level 2 [84].
- **Hunga Tonga, 2022:** the explosive scale was not anticipated. A precursor signal was recognised only in hindsight [85].

**Machine learning**
- Dempsey et al. used a single seismic station to anticipate 4 of 5 Whakaari eruptions in retrospective tests [86].
- Ardid et al. (2025) trained on 41 eruptions at 24 volcanoes. Their models forecast eruptions at volcanoes not in the training set with skill comparable to locally trained models [87].
- These methods are not yet proven in operational use [86][87].

**Coverage**
- **Global:** about 600 of 1,302 Holocene volcanoes (~46%) have at least one monitoring station, and only about 300 (~23%) have two or more methods (data to August 2023) [89].
- **Historically active volcanoes:** 25–45% are unmonitored (Brown et al., 2015) [90].
- **Latin America:** more than 60% of Holocene volcanoes are unmonitored [91].
- **United States:**
  - 161 volcanoes are assessed, 18 of them rated very high threat [92][94].
  - USGS says "most U.S. volcanoes are currently under-monitored" [81].
  - The 2018 threat assessment is still being updated [94].
- **Alert-level schemes are not standardised.** USGS, JMA, GeoNet and IMO each use different levels [83][84][18].

- The top 10 hold more than 93% of capacity [95]:

- **Global share:** geothermal meets about 1% of world electricity demand [96]. The IEA says next-generation geothermal could supply up to 15% of demand growth to 2050 [96].
- **National shares:**
  - Kenya: 39.8% (2024, regulator EPRA) or 45.9% (EIA-based data) [97][98].
  - Iceland: about 30% [99].
  - New Zealand: about 20% [100].
- **Next-generation projects:**
  - Fervo Energy's Cape Station in Utah declared commercial operation of its first 33 MW block in October 2026. It is described as the first greenfield enhanced geothermal system (EGS) plant to reach commercial operation, and 500 MW is planned by 2028 [101].
  - Iceland's Krafla Magma Testbed plans to drill into a shallow rhyolitic magma body. No confirmation was found that drilling had begun [102].

**Volcanic soils (andisols)**
- Andisols cover about 1% of ice-free land [103][104].
- One review estimates they support about 10% of the world's population, for example on Java [104].
- They are fertile because they hold water and nutrients well and young ash keeps weathering [103].
- Their drawback is strong phosphorus fixation [103].

**Mineral resources**
- **Copper:** porphyry deposits formed in volcanic arcs supply about 60% of world copper [105].
- **Sulfur:** still hand-mined at Kawah Ijen under hazardous conditions [106].
- **Lithium:**
  - Thacker Pass in Nevada is a volcanic-caldera clay deposit. First production is targeted for late 2026, after repeated delays [107].
  - Salton Sea geothermal brines hold an estimated 4.1–18 Mt of lithium carbonate equivalent [108].

**Tourism**
- **Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park:** about 1.43 million visits and US$445 million in local spending in 2024 [109].
- **Bromo:** about 369,000 visitors in 2023 [110].
- **Iceland:** eruptions both attract and deter visitors [111].
- **Etna:** has no official count of its economic impact [112].
- **Risks:** Whakaari, Ontake, Marapi and Dukono show the cost when access is not tied to hazard status [36][57][58][83].

### 7. Supervolcanoes, large-eruption risk, policy and research frontiers

**Supervolcanoes are a low near-term threat**
- **Yellowstone:**
  - USGS puts the annual chance of a caldera-forming eruption at about 1 in 730,000 [113].
  - Magnetotelluric imaging in 2025 found melt fractions of at most about 18%. That is well below the roughly 40% generally treated as eruptible [114].
- **Long Valley:** a crystallised "lid" sits over its chamber [115].
- **Taupō:** brief unrest in 2022–23 subsided [116].
- **Toba:** models suggest eruptible magma can build up quietly over long periods [117].

**VEI 7 is the bigger concern**
- Ice-core records imply a magnitude-7 eruption about every 625 years, roughly a 1-in-6 chance per century [118].
- Cassidy and Mani (2022) note that only 27% of eruptions since 1950 had a nearby seismometer. They warn of possible losses in the trillions and call for better risk mapping and a dedicated volcano-monitoring satellite [118].
- Lloyd's 2024 scenario for a VEI-6-class eruption near a city gives a US$1.6 trillion five-year global loss [119].

**Policy and governance**
- **Sendai Framework and Early Warnings for All:** neither has volcano-specific targets [133].
- **US National Volcano Early Warning System (NVEWS):**
  - Enacted in 2019 with US$55 million authorised for FY2019–23. Appropriations never reached that level [92].
  - Reauthorisation bills were pending in 2025 [92].
  - The FY2026 budget request proposed a 30% cut to USGS hazards. Congress rejected it and appropriated US$200.1 million, but the FY2027 request repeats the cut [93].
- **USGS–USAID Volcano Disaster Assistance Program (VDAP):** USAID funded it "almost entirely" [129], so its future looked uncertain after USAID was dismantled in 2025 [128]. It is still operating in 2026: it published a Peru data release in January and did fieldwork in El Salvador in March [130][131]. Its funding source is not publicly documented [132].

**Campi Flegrei, the most active national response**
- **Two separate zones:**
  - The volcanic red zone holds about 500,000 people. The yellow (ash-fall) zone holds more than 800,000 [122].
  - A separate bradyseism (ground uplift and earthquake) intervention zone holds about 85,000 people [123].
- **Evacuation plan:**
  - Red-zone residents would be evacuated within 72 hours of an alarm: 12 h of preparation, 48 h of departures and 12 h of reserve [122][124].
  - Each municipality is twinned with an Italian region that would host its evacuees [122][124].
  - The national plan is still awaiting formal final adoption [124].
- **Exercises:** 2019, 2024 (with low public turnout) and November 2025 [125][127].
- **Alert levels:** a decree of 30 October 2025 created a six-state scheme. The area is currently at Yellow Phase 2 [126].
- **Funding:** after the July 2026 earthquake, a decree-law added €100 million and raised the state's share of building-retrofit costs to 70% [121].

**Research frontiers**
- **Magma storage:** crystal-mush versus melt-lens storage models [114][137].
- **Imaging:** new methods such as magnetotellurics and fibre-optic sensing [114][115].
- **Climate link:** ice unloading after deglaciation produced larger and more explosive eruptions in Chile [136].
- **Volcanic tsunamis:** the Hunga Tonga wave was about 90 m high at its origin. Volcanic-tsunami warning is described as decades behind earthquake-tsunami warning [134][135].
- **Machine-learning forecasting:** see section 5 [87].
- **Drilling into magma:** the Krafla Magma Testbed [102].
- **Mitigation:** Cassidy and Mani propose research into countering volcanic aerosols and into manipulating magma. These ideas remain speculative [118].

## Points of disagreement

- **Exposure figures:** about 800 million (GVM, 2011 data) versus more than 1 billion (Freire et al., 2015 data) within 100 km. The difference comes from different years, volcano lists and methods [52][53].
- **Hazard rankings:** the PDC share of deaths ranges from 21% to 33% depending on the period covered and whether indirect famine and disease deaths are counted [48][49][50].
- **Pinatubo lives saved:** 5,000 (USGS) versus 20,000+ (a PHIVOLCS account). The cooling peak is given as 0.5 °C or about 0.7 °C [56][62][63].
- **Hunga Tonga climate effect:** the water-vapour-only model gave warming, while full observational accounting gives cooling [67][68]. SO₂ estimates are 0.4–0.5 Tg in most studies but above 1 Tg in one IASI retrieval [69].
- **Eruptions per year:** the popular "50–70" figure versus GVP's measured 63–66 volcanoes and 71–74 eruptions. Better satellite detection inflates recent counts [2].
- **Monitoring coverage:** about 46% of Holocene volcanoes have any station, 25–45% of historically active volcanoes are unmonitored, and 27% of eruptions had a nearby seismometer. These measure different things and do not contradict each other [89][90][118].
- **Campi Flegrei population:** "80,000" versus "500,000" refers to two different plans (bradyseism versus eruption) [122][123].
- **Event details:**
  - Lewotobi deaths: 9 or 10 [24].
  - Dukono injured: 3 or 5 [36][37].
  - Etna flight cancellations: about 388 or about 700 [22][23].
  - Kanlaon Permanent Danger Zone: 4 km or 6 km [29].
  - Kenya's geothermal share: 39.8% or 45.9% [97][98].
  - Campi Flegrei injuries in July 2026: 8 to 21 [43].

## Open questions and limitations

- **Iceland forecasting:** it is unclear whether the Svartsengi volume method has lost skill or the system is waning. No IMO revision of the method was found [17].
- **VDAP funding:** USGS budget documents, the agency's own site and the press were searched, and none says who funds VDAP after USAID [130][132].
- **Krafla Magma Testbed:** sources say drilling starts in 2026 or by 2027. No confirmation of a start was found [102].
- **Campi Flegrei plan:** the national red-zone plan has not been formally adopted. The full twinning table and the text of the alert-decree annexes could not be retrieved [124].
- **Monitoring data:** no global figure isolates continuous telemetered monitoring. The US threat-assessment update is unpublished [89][94].
- **Fatalities and losses:** there is no global fatality database after 2017, and economic loss data are weak. For example, Merapi 2010 losses are quoted at US$300 million and US$3.12 billion [60].
- **2026 data:** full-year 2026 eruption statistics are not yet available, and GVP data runs to 31 March 2026 [2].
- **Event status after mid-2026:** some events rest on news reporting for their latest status: Kanlaon in October 2026, the Catania airport reopening date, and whether Titan Ridge has ended [23][29][38].
- **Some secondary sources:** the VEI thresholds, the andisol population share and Laki livestock losses come from single or secondary sources [13][76][104].
- **Method:** the research ran through web search and fetch. Several primary sites (GVP and MDPI) blocked direct fetches. GVP data was obtained through browser rendering in a follow-up round.
